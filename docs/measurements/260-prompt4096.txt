The town of Halstrand lies at the head of a long glacial valley, two thousand metres above the sea and a full day's drive from the nearest city, on a shelf of flat ground where the valley floor stops climbing and the mountains close in on three sides. It was founded for the silver in the rock behind it, and for a hundred and fifty years silver was the only reason anyone lived there. The mine is closed now, its headframe preserved as a monument and its tunnels sealed, and the town survives on a stranger combination of trades: an astronomical observatory on the ridge above it, a small but well-known school of stone carving, a hydroelectric station in the gorge below, and the steady trickle of walkers and climbers who come in summer for the high passes and in winter for the frozen waterfalls. Perhaps nine hundred people live there through the year, and a few hundred more in the warm months. The valley is so narrow at the town that the sun does not reach the main street for six weeks either side of midwinter, and the first morning in February when light touches the clock on the old mine office is marked, half seriously, with a small procession and a cup of hot wine for anyone who turns out.
The town is laid out along a single road that follows the river, and most of what it has is on that road. At the lower end, where the valley is widest, stand the hydroelectric station, the bus turning circle and a cluster of newer houses with large windows and steep metal roofs built for the observatory staff. The road then crosses the river on an iron bridge and enters the old town, which was built in a hurry in the first decades of the mine and shows it: the houses are tall and narrow and crammed together, their lower storeys of stone and their upper storeys of timber, with wooden balconies that overhang the street and nearly meet in places. Here are the two inns, the bakery, the general store that is also the post office and the pharmacy, the carving school in what was once the mine company's assay house, and the church, which is the only building in the town with any pretension to grandeur, having been paid for by the mine's owners in a good year and furnished with a carved altar that the school's students still study. Above the old town the road becomes a track, climbs through a stand of larch and ends at the mine itself, with its stone-built offices, its preserved headframe, its spoil heaps now grassed over and used by the children for sledging, and the small museum that occupies the former powder store. Beyond that there is only the path to the observatory, which zigzags up the open mountainside for an hour and a half, and the passes.
The climate is severe and the people speak of it with something close to pride. Snow lies on the ground from November to April, and in the old town it is not cleared from the street so much as trodden into a hard floor that rises through the winter until, by March, the ground-floor windows are at knee height. The river freezes along its edges but never entirely, and the sound of it is the constant background to every winter night. Avalanches close the road below the town several times each winter, sometimes for a day and sometimes for a week, and the town keeps a store of food, fuel and medicine against those closures that is inspected each autumn by a committee of three whose office is older than the mine. Spring arrives as a sudden violence of meltwater, when the river rises to the arch of the iron bridge and every gully on the mountainside runs white, and for a month the town is loud and muddy and short-tempered. Summer is brief and extraordinarily clear. The air at that height is thin enough that the sky is a darker blue than visitors expect, the sun burns quickly, and the nights, which are the reason for the observatory, are so black and still that the Milky Way casts a visible shadow on the snow that lingers in the high corries.
The observatory was built in the nineteen-twenties, when the mine was already in decline and the town's council, looking for anything that might keep people in the valley, offered the site on the ridge free of rent to a university that was searching for dark skies and dry air. The first instrument was a modest refractor housed in a wooden dome that still stands, now used as a store. The present observatory consists of four domes, a long low building of offices and dormitories, and a radio dish that was added in the nineteen-seventies and which the town's children, for reasons no one can now reconstruct, call the ear. Twenty or so people work there, some for a season and some for decades, and they form a distinct community within the town, recognisable by their habit of sleeping in the day and by the red lights they carry at night to protect their eyes. The relationship between the observatory and the town is affectionate but has its rules. The street lamps of Halstrand are shielded and dimmed after ten in the evening, the inns draw their curtains, and anyone who drives up the mine track at night with headlights on will be spoken to the next morning. In return the observatory opens its smallest dome to the public every clear Saturday in summer, and once a year, in September, holds an evening at which the astronomers set up telescopes in the square and the whole town comes out in coats and blankets to look at whatever is on offer.
The carving school is the town's other institution and in some ways the more surprising one. It grew out of the mine, which employed masons to dress stone for its buildings and discovered, in the grey-green rock of the valley, a material that took fine detail and weathered slowly. When the mine began to shed workers at the end of the nineteenth century, one of its masons set up a workshop in the assay house and took on apprentices, and his workshop, through a sequence of accidents and strong personalities, became a school with a reputation that reaches well beyond the valley. It has perhaps forty students at a time, who stay for two or three years and who are recognisable in the town by the dust on their clothes and the state of their hands. They learn lettering first, then ornament, then figure work, and the evidence of their learning is all over Halstrand: in the headstones in the churchyard, each of which was cut by a student as a graduation piece, in the fountain in the square, in the lintels above the doors of the old town, many of which carry a small carved animal or flower added by some student decades ago and now worn soft by the weather. The school supports itself partly by commissions and partly by selling its students' work to the summer visitors, and the relationship between the students, who are young and come from everywhere, and the town, which is old and comes from nowhere else, is a constant source of friction and marriage in roughly equal measure.
The hydroelectric station in the gorge is the quietest of the town's employers and the one that keeps the lights on, in both senses. It was built in the nineteen-fifties on the site of an earlier water mill that had ground ore for the mine, and it takes the river through a tunnel in the rock and drops it two hundred metres onto a pair of turbines that supply the town, the observatory and the villages further down the valley, with a surplus sold to the grid. Eight people run it, in shifts, and they are the only inhabitants of Halstrand with a direct telephone line to the outside world that does not depend on the single cable that follows the road, which the avalanches cut with some regularity. In the hardest winters the station's control room becomes, informally, the town's emergency centre, and the engineers have on more than one occasion coordinated the digging out of the road with the regional authority while the rest of the town was without power or telephone.
Food in Halstrand is a matter of what can be grown in a short season, what can be kept, and what can be carried up the road. The meadows above the town are cut for hay once in August and grazed by a few dozen cattle and a larger number of goats, and the goats' milk goes into a hard, sharp cheese that is made in the autumn and eaten through the winter. Potatoes, cabbages and onions grow in the gardens behind the old town, which are small and walled and face south, and there is a long tradition of rivalry over whose garden produces the first new potatoes of the year. Everything else comes by lorry, and the general store's prices rise visibly in the weeks after a long road closure. The bakery is the town's social centre in winter, as the square is in summer; its ovens are lit at four in the morning, and the astronomers coming down from the ridge at the end of a night's observing and the hydro engineers coming off the night shift meet there for the first bread of the day. The two inns divide the town between them along lines that no one outside the valley could explain, and it is said, not entirely in jest, that a marriage between a family from one inn and a family from the other is a greater event than a marriage between a local and a stranger.
Death and memory have a particular place in a town where the ground is frozen for half the year. Those who die in winter are kept in a stone vault in the churchyard until the thaw, and the funerals of the winter dead are held together in a single week of May, which is both the saddest and, in its way, the most convivial week in the calendar, since families who have not seen each other since the autumn come up the valley for it and stay. The mine's own dead, of whom there were many, are remembered on a wall of names in the old powder store, and on the anniversary of the worst accident, a fall of rock in the eighteen-eighties that killed thirty-one men in an afternoon, the church bell is rung thirty-one times at the hour it happened. The carving students of each year are given, as their first serious lettering exercise, the task of recutting a portion of that wall where the names have begun to weather, so that the list is never allowed to fade and every student in the school's history has had a hand in keeping it.
The children of Halstrand attend a primary school of thirty pupils in a building next to the church, and at eleven they go down the valley to a boarding school in the market town, coming home at weekends when the road is open and staying away when it is not. This arrangement is universally disliked and has never been changed, because there are not enough children to justify a secondary school and no family is willing to leave. Most of the town's young people leave at eighteen, for the city or the coast, and a surprising number come back in their thirties with a trade, a spouse and a conviction that nowhere else is quite right. The town's population has been stable within a few dozen for half a century, which is a kind of achievement in a mountain valley, and the council, which meets in the old mine office under the clock, attributes it to the fact that Halstrand has never had a single employer since the mine and has therefore never had a single thing to lose.
The walkers and climbers who come in summer are tolerated, welcomed and quietly managed. They are housed in the two inns, in a hostel in a former mine barracks, and in a campsite in the meadow below the hydro station, and they are steered by a network of signs, leaflets and casual remarks away from the places the town would prefer they did not go: the sealed adits of the mine, which are dangerous, the observatory ridge at night, which is forbidden, and a particular high lake above the passes which the town has decided, without ever saying so, to keep for itself. The mountain rescue team is drawn from the hydro engineers, the carving students and a handful of farmers, and it goes out perhaps fifteen times a summer, most often for sprained ankles and lost parties on the passes, and once or twice for something worse. Its equipment store, its radio and its training are paid for by the proceeds of the September star-gazing evening, which charges nothing for admission but sells a great deal of hot wine.
In winter the visitors are fewer and stranger: ice climbers who come for the frozen waterfalls in the gorge, photographers who come for the light, and a certain number of people who come, as far as anyone can tell, simply to be somewhere very quiet and very cold. The inns are glad of them. The town, which has a long memory for how it was before the observatory and the school, and which knows how easily a mountain valley empties, takes their money without resentment and their company without much curiosity, and lets them go in the spring. It is, by its own account, not a friendly town and not an unfriendly one, but a place that has decided what it is and sees no reason to explain, and the visitor who stays long enough to be told the story of the thirty-one bells, or to be shown the lintel that a particular carver cut for a particular door, has been given something that Halstrand does not give lightly.
The history of the mine is told in the museum in the powder store, and it is a history that the town has edited over the years into something it can live with. Silver was found in the valley, according to the account on the museum's first panel, by a shepherd who noticed the glint of ore in a stream bed after a flood, though the museum's own curator, a retired schoolteacher who has read the company's ledgers, will tell any visitor who asks that the deposit was known to prospectors for a generation before the shepherd and that the story was invented by the company to make the discovery seem providential. The first shaft was sunk in the seventeen-nineties by a partnership of merchants from the plain, who built the road, the bridge and the first stone houses and who brought in miners from a district far to the east whose descendants still carry its surnames. The mine was profitable for a century and enormously so for two decades in the middle of it, when a new vein was struck and the town doubled in size in five years, acquiring its church, its square and its reputation for hard drinking. After that the ore grew poorer and the workings deeper, the water in the lower levels became unmanageable, and the company passed through a series of owners each less willing than the last to spend money on pumps. The last shift came up in the nineteen-thirties. The museum has the company's final wage book open at the last page, and the names in it are the names on the doors of the old town.
The road is the town's lifeline and its great anxiety. It was cut by the mine company along the only line the valley offered, a ledge above the river that is exposed to avalanche in three places and to rockfall in two more, and in the two centuries since it was built it has been widened, surfaced, fitted with galleries at the worst avalanche crossings and furnished with a sequence of barriers and warning lights, without ever becoming a road that anyone drives at night in winter with an easy mind. A bus comes up it twice a day in summer and once in winter, when the road is open, and the arrival of the bus is still, in a town with its own cars and its own satellite dish, an event at which people gather. The driver is a woman from the market town who has driven the route for twenty years and who is credited with a judgement of snow conditions that the regional authority's instruments do not match; when she declines to drive, the road is closed, whatever the instruments say. The council has for decades argued for a tunnel that would bypass the worst section, and for decades the regional authority has replied that the cost cannot be justified for nine hundred people, and the argument has acquired the comfortable permanence of a feud that neither side expects to win.
The church is the centre of the town's music, which is more than a town of its size has any right to. The mine's miners from the east brought with them a tradition of unaccompanied singing in parts, and the mine company, which found that singing miners drank less than silent ones, encouraged it with a hall and a conductor. The choir that resulted outlived the mine and is now the principal reason that the church is full on the feast days, since the town's religious observance is otherwise moderate. It sings at the May funerals, at the September star-gazing, at the first sunlight in February, and at a Christmas service that the observatory's astronomers, whatever their beliefs, attend as a body because the choir has for generations sung a hymn about the stars in their honour. The carved altar, which the carving students study, shows the mountains of the valley with the sun rising over them and a line of small figures, miners with lamps, walking into the light; it was cut in the good years by a mason whose name is lost, and the students argue, as students will, about whether the figures are walking into heaven or merely into the morning shift.
The observatory's work has changed with the century. The dark skies and dry air that brought the university to the ridge are still there, but the big telescopes of the world have long since moved to higher and drier places on other continents, and the domes above Halstrand now do steady, unglamorous work that suits a small team: the monitoring of variable stars over decades, the follow-up of objects that larger instruments have found and cannot afford to watch, and, through the ear, the tracking of a handful of distant spacecraft whose signals need a dish that is pointed at them for hours on end. The astronomers describe their work to the town at the September evening in terms the town has come to enjoy, and the town's children grow up with a knowledge of the sky that surprises their teachers down the valley. Two of the present observatory staff were born in Halstrand, went away to study and came back, which the town regards as the natural order of things and the university as a minor miracle of recruitment.
From the top of the pass above the observatory, on a clear day, it is possible to see the whole arrangement at once: the town along its river like beads on a thread, the ridge with its domes and its ear, the gorge with the station hidden in it, the scar of the mine with its grassed-over heaps, and below everything the valley opening out, green and then blue and then grey, to the plain and the distant city that most of the town's children will one day leave for and some will come back from. The path down from the pass takes the better part of an afternoon, and by the time it reaches the larches above the mine the light has usually left the valley floor and the first lamps are being lit in the old town, shielded and dim as the observatory requires, so that from above the street looks less like a town than like a row of embers along the dark line of the river, with the single brighter window of the bakery, where the ovens are already being banked for the morning, marking the middle of it. It is a small place with a great deal in it, and the people who live there are, on the whole, aware of that, and prefer not to say so, except, now and then, to a visitor who has stayed long enough to have properly earned it.
